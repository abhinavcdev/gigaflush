Starting a project used to take an afternoon. Now it takes one prompt. Ask Cursor, Claude Code, Copilot or a browser builder for "a habit tracker with auth" and a minute later you have a working Next.js app, and a fresh `node_modules` folder sitting on your disk.

That's the part nobody mentions. **The code is a few hundred kilobytes. The dependencies are hundreds of megabytes.** Do that every evening for a month and your "Storage almost full" warning arrives right on schedule.

## Every idea ships with its own dependencies

Try it. Each click below is one weekend idea, scaffolded the usual way:

<ProjectPileUp client:visible />

Nothing here is wrong. `node_modules`, Rust's `target`, Flutter's `build` and friends are supposed to be big: they hold every dependency and every compiled artifact so your next build is fast. The problem is that they **stay** after you've moved on. Most vibe-coded projects are opened a handful of times and then forgotten, along with everything they installed.

## Why it adds up faster than you'd think

- **No sharing between projects.** With npm and Yarn Classic, every project gets its own full copy of `react`, `typescript`, `esbuild` and the rest. Ten Next.js apps means ten copies.
- **Native binaries.** Modern JavaScript tooling ships compiled binaries for your platform: SWC, esbuild, Lightningcss, Sharp. They're fast, and they're not small.
- **Rust and Flutter are worse.** A Rust `target` folder keeps debug builds, release builds and an incremental-compilation cache. A Flutter app keeps build output for every platform you've run it on.
- **Clones and worktrees.** Agents love git worktrees and fresh clones. Each one is a new place for dependencies to be installed.

## How much is yours?

Rough per-project averages hide a lot of variation, but they're useful for a gut check. Slide in your own numbers:

<SpaceCalculator client:visible />

To see the real figure on macOS or Linux, run this from your projects folder:

```bash
find . -name node_modules -type d -prune -exec du -sh {} + | sort -h
```

<Callout type="tip" title="Use -prune">
  Without `-prune`, `find` walks into every `node_modules` and also lists the nested ones inside it, which is slow and double-counts.
</Callout>

## Getting the space back

You have three options, from most manual to least:

1. **Delete by hand.** `rm -rf some-project/node_modules`, project by project. Fine for two folders, tedious for forty.
2. **One big `find … -exec rm -rf`.** Fast, but it's a blind delete: no review, no size, and an easy way to hit something you didn't mean to. See [how to delete all node_modules folders safely](/blog/delete-all-node-modules) for the careful version.
3. **Use a tool that shows you first.** That's why we built [Gigaflush](/): it scans a folder, lists every rebuildable folder across Node, Rust, Flutter, Python, Swift, Gradle and Maven, sorted by size, and only removes the ones you tick after showing you the exact paths.

Whatever you use, deleting these folders is safe as long as the project can reinstall them. Your source code, lockfiles and git history aren't touched, and `npm install` (or `cargo build`, or `flutter pub get`) brings everything back the next time you open the project.

## Habits that keep it under control

- **Prefer pnpm for new JavaScript projects.** pnpm keeps one copy of each package version in a global store and links it into projects, so ten apps don't mean ten copies.
- **Share a Cargo target directory** for small Rust experiments by setting `CARGO_TARGET_DIR`. Read more in our [Rust target folder guide](/blog/rust-target-folder-size).
- **Archive what you're done with.** Push it to GitHub, then delete the local generated folders, or the whole clone.
- **Do a monthly sweep.** Point Gigaflush (or your `find` command) at your projects folder once a month and clear out anything you haven't touched.
- **Check for servers the agent left running.** Agents start `vite` and `next dev` to test their work and often never stop them. They hold ports and memory for days. See [Port 3000 already in use?](/blog/port-already-in-use-ghost-dev-servers)

AI made starting projects nearly free. Cleaning up after them can be just as cheap.
